How to Make a Study Timetable You Can Actually Follow

A timetable should help you study—not make you feel guilty. The best plan is realistic, includes breaks, gives difficult subjects enough attention, and can adapt when life does not go exactly as expected.

1. Start with your real commitments

Write down the things that already take up time: school or classes, travel, meals, sleep, family responsibilities, exercise, and other important activities. Put these into your weekly calendar first. Then plan study sessions around them.

Do not plan every free minute. Leave some unscheduled time for unexpected homework, difficult questions, or rest. If your plan only works on a perfect day, it is probably too strict.

প্রথমে স্কুল, যাতায়াত, খাওয়া, ঘুম ও পরিবারের কাজের সময় লিখে নাও। তারপর অবশিষ্ট সময় থেকে পড়ার সময় ঠিক করো। প্রতিটি মিনিট ভরে ফেলবে না—হঠাৎ কাজের জন্য কিছু সময় খালি রাখবে।

2. Choose clear priorities, not just subjects

A list such as “English, maths, science” is not specific enough. Give each session a clear task: “solve five ratio problems,” “write a paragraph from memory,” or “review ten vocabulary cards.” A clear task makes it easier to begin and to tell whether you finished.

Prioritise by asking: What is due soon? What do I find difficult? What needs more practice? You do not need to give every subject equal time every day. Give urgent or challenging work enough attention while keeping other subjects in rotation.

Instead of: “Study science for one hour.”
Try: “Review the water cycle, draw it from memory, and answer five questions.”

3. Plan focused study blocks and breaks

Choose a session length that suits your age, task, and attention. You could start with 25 minutes of focused work followed by a 5-minute break, or use a shorter block if starting feels difficult. After several blocks, take a longer break. These are options, not strict rules.

During a study block, keep only the materials you need nearby and silence non-essential notifications. During a break, stand up, stretch, drink water, or rest your eyes. If a task needs more time than expected, adjust the next block instead of treating the whole day as a failure.

4. Make room for revision and practice

Do not reserve all your time for new lessons. Add short review sessions for older topics and time to answer questions without looking at notes. When you make a mistake, record what went wrong and revisit that skill later.

For example, after learning a topic today, spend a few minutes testing yourself tomorrow, then return to it again later in the week. If you keep forgetting it, review it sooner. This is more useful than waiting until the night before an exam to revisit everything.

If your school day ends later, shift the plan. You can use one focused block on a busy day and longer sessions on a lighter day. Protect enough time for sleep; your timetable should not depend on regularly sacrificing it.

6. Review and adjust each week

At the end of the week, ask: Which sessions did I complete? Which tasks took longer than expected? What distracted me? Which subject needs more practice next week? Change the plan based on what actually happened—not on what you hoped would happen.

  • If you often skip a session: make it shorter or move it to a better time.
  • If a subject feels overwhelming: split it into smaller tasks.
  • If the plan is too easy: add a practice question or review block.
  • If you fall behind: choose the most important unfinished task and restart; do not punish yourself by doubling every session.

Common timetable mistakes

  • Planning every minute with no buffer time.
  • Writing vague goals instead of specific tasks.
  • Ignoring breaks, meals, exercise, or sleep.
  • Spending all study time rereading and never testing yourself.
  • Copying another student’s routine without considering your own commitments.

Frequently asked questions

How many hours should a student study every day?

There is no single number that fits every student. It depends on age, school workload, upcoming exams, learning needs, and responsibilities. Focus on completing useful tasks consistently rather than chasing a high number of hours.

Should I study the same subjects every day?

Some regular review can help, but rotate subjects based on deadlines, difficulty, and what needs practice. Keep a short review habit and make sure no important subject disappears from your plan for too long.

What should I do if I miss my timetable?

Restart with the next planned task. If you miss sessions repeatedly, reduce the plan or move them. A timetable is a tool to guide you, not a test of your worth.

Is a digital timetable better than a paper timetable?

Neither is automatically better. Use whichever you can check easily and update consistently. Paper may be less distracting; a digital calendar may be easier to rearrange.
